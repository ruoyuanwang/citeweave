# Climate Change Research 1990–2021: A Comprehensive Bibliometric Analysis of Performance, Social Structure, and Conceptual Evolution

## Structured Abstract

**Background:** Climate change research has expanded dramatically over the past three decades, yet systematic characterizations of its bibliometric structures remain fragmented. This study provides a reproducible, evidence-based mapping of the performance, social organization, and conceptual landscape of climate change scholarship from 1990 to 2021.

**Methods:** A corpus of 151,562 unique journal articles was acquired from a bibliographic source, achieving 99.6% completeness relative to the source-reported result set. Performance analysis included descriptive productivity and citation metrics, Bradford zoning, and temporal trend analysis. Social structure was examined through coauthorship and institution collaboration networks. Conceptual and intellectual structures were mapped via keyword co-occurrence, bibliographic coupling, cocitation, and direct citation networks, all employing association-strength normalization and Louvain community detection.

**Results:** Annual output grew from 127 publications in 1990 to a peak of 19,170 in 2021, representing a compound annual growth rate of 17.57%. The citation distribution is highly skewed (mean 49.56, median 14.00), with 19.39% of works remaining uncited. *Climatic Change* is the most productive journal (2,093 publications), while the Chinese Academy of Sciences leads institutional output (3,468 publications). Network analyses reveal a densely interconnected conceptual core dominated by "Climate change," "Geography," and "Environmental science," with distinct thematic clusters spanning ecology, economics, and public health. Intellectual structure is anchored by foundational IPCC assessment reports and landmark studies on food security, biodiversity, and species distribution modeling. The social structure features a dominant, highly collaborative author community centered on agricultural and economic modeling, alongside a polycentric institutional network.

**Conclusions:** Climate change research exhibits hyperexponential growth, a concentrated core of journals and institutions, and a mature conceptual structure that has integrated diverse disciplinary perspectives. The field's intellectual foundations remain strongly tied to intergovernmental assessment processes, while emerging frontiers in health impacts, climate finance, and renewable energy transitions signal ongoing thematic diversification.

## 1. Introduction

Climate change represents one of the most pressing challenges of the twenty-first century, driving an unprecedented mobilization of scientific inquiry across the natural, social, and health sciences. Understanding the structure and evolution of this vast research enterprise is essential for researchers, funders, and policymakers seeking to identify knowledge gaps, foster interdisciplinary collaboration, and accelerate evidence-informed action. Bibliometric analysis offers systematic, reproducible methods for mapping the performance, social organization, and intellectual architecture of scientific fields [E069].

This study presents a comprehensive bibliometric analysis of climate change research published between 1990 and 2021. Drawing on a rigorously curated corpus of 151,562 unique journal articles [E001], we integrate performance metrics, science mapping, and network analysis to characterize the field's growth trajectory, key contributors, collaborative patterns, and conceptual evolution. The analysis is guided by established bibliometric frameworks [E069, E070] and employs community detection and visualization techniques grounded in the VOSviewer methodology [E071, E072].

The report is organized as follows: Section 2 details the reproducible methods employed. Section 3 presents integrated findings across performance, social, conceptual, and intellectual dimensions. Section 4 discusses the implications of these structures, and Section 5 addresses limitations. Section 6 concludes with a synthesis of the field's current state and future directions.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Corpus Acquisition and Quality Assurance

The bibliographic corpus was acquired through cursor-based pagination from a single source, continuing until no further results were returned. The source reported 152,599 expected results; 152,030 unique source records were successfully acquired, representing 99.6% completeness with no truncation [E002]. After deterministic exact deduplication, the canonical corpus comprised 151,562 unique works [E001].

Metadata completeness was assessed for all canonical works. Critical fields demonstrated high coverage: publication year, document type, cited-by count, reference count, and source identifier were present for 100% of records. Title coverage was 99.96%, language 99.5%, and abstract 74.73%. Digital object identifiers (DOIs) were available for 80.8% of works, and publisher information for 79.79% [E003]. All analyses were conducted on the canonical corpus using whole counting unless otherwise specified.

### 2.2 Performance Analysis

Annual publication output was computed by grouping works by non-missing publication year. Compound annual growth rate (CAGR) was calculated as (final year count / initial year count)^(1 / year difference) − 1, using non-zero endpoints [E006]. Citation metrics were derived from source-reported cited-by counts at the time of retrieval. Bradford's law of scattering was applied by ranking sources by publication count and partitioning cumulative documents into equal thirds, with sources lacking a name excluded from zoning [E019].

### 2.3 Network Construction and Analysis

Five network types were constructed to map social, conceptual, and intellectual structures: coauthorship (author–author), institution collaboration (institution–institution), keyword co-occurrence (keyword–keyword), bibliographic coupling (document–document via shared references), cocitation (reference–reference via co-citation in citing documents), and direct citation (document–document via citation links). All networks employed association-strength normalization (c_ij / (w_i × w_j)) and Louvain community detection [E072], with small clusters merged into larger communities. Candidate selection thresholds varied by network type: coauthorship required a minimum of 30 occurrences, institution collaboration 145, keyword co-occurrence 513, bibliographic coupling 10, cocitation 261, and direct citation 456 [E022–E027]. Edge reduction was performed using maximum-spanning forest plus the strongest edges per node, with a maximum of four edges per node displayed. Layout algorithms included VOS-style association-strength community layouts and ForceAtlas2 LinLog, with multiple deterministic restarts to optimize convergence [E022–E027].

### 2.4 Visualization

Figures were rendered deterministically from analysis outputs. Network visualizations encode node size by winsorized square-root occurrence and color by Louvain community membership. Temporal overlays on keyword networks display mean publication year per node. Density visualizations employ Gaussian kernel density estimation with log(1 + occurrence) weighting [E067, E068].

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Performance Structure: Growth, Productivity, and Impact

The corpus spans 1990 to 2021 [E004], a period of extraordinary expansion in climate change scholarship. Annual output increased from 127 publications in 1990 to 19,170 in 2021, the peak year [E005, E007]. This trajectory corresponds to a compound annual growth rate of 17.57% [E006], reflecting sustained, accelerating interest. Growth was not monotonic; the largest absolute year-over-year increases occurred in the most recent period, with gains of 3,010 publications in 2020 and 2,442 in 2021 [E007]. The final year may be incomplete due to retrieval timing [E005], yet the upward trend is unambiguous.

The corpus consists entirely of journal articles (100%) [E014], indicating that the source classification system does not distinguish reviews or other document types within this dataset. This homogeneity simplifies interpretation but limits analysis of review-versus-primary research dynamics.

Citation impact is highly skewed. The mean cited-by count is 49.56, while the median is only 14.00 [E015], indicating that a small fraction of highly cited works elevates the average. The 75th percentile is 46 citations, the 90th percentile 110, and the 95th percentile 186; the maximum observed citation count is 22,581 [E017]. Notably, 29,384 works (19.39%) have received zero source-reported citations [E016], a proportion that partly reflects the recency of many publications, as newer works have had less time to accumulate citations [E016 caveat].

The most-cited documents reveal the field's foundational contributions. The highest-cited work, with 22,581 citations, is a 2008 *Lancet* publication on newborn survival [E018]. Other highly cited works include "Food Security: The Challenge of Feeding 9 Billion People" (2010, 11,854 citations) [E018, E028], "A globally coherent fingerprint of climate change impacts across natural systems" (2003, 11,212 citations), and "Ecological responses to recent climate change" (2002, 9,973 citations) [E018]. These works span food systems, biodiversity, and detection-attribution science, illustrating the multidisciplinary nature of high-impact climate research.

### 3.2 Social Structure: Authors, Institutions, and Collaboration

The social organization of climate change research is characterized by a dominant, extraordinarily prolific author and a polycentric institutional landscape. AGB Poore ranks first with 5,808 publications, an output that far exceeds the second-ranked author, Kristie L. Ebi (150 publications) [E010, E011]. This extreme concentration reflects Poore's association with a large repository-based dataset (AgEcon Search), as evidenced by the three-field analysis showing 721 documents linking Poore, the keyword "Economics," and AgEcon Search [E021]. The remaining top authors include established climate change researchers such as James D. Ford (138 publications), Philippe Ciais (133), Richard S.J. Tol (127), and Cynthia Rosenzweig (112) [E011].

Institutionally, the Chinese Academy of Sciences leads with 3,468 publications, followed by the Centre National de la Recherche Scientifique (CNRS, 2,564) and the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences (1,524) [E012, E013]. The presence of three Chinese institutions among the top fifteen, alongside major European, North American, and Australian research organizations, indicates a geographically distributed but concentrated production system.

The coauthorship network (Figure 10) contains 60 displayed nodes and 149 edges, organized into 10 Louvain communities from a candidate pool of 431 authors with at least 30 occurrences [E024, E061]. AGB Poore occupies a distinct community (Cluster 6) with a weighted degree of 860, reflecting extensive but structurally separate collaboration patterns [E024]. A second prominent community (Cluster 1) centers on agricultural modelers including Christoph Müller (weighted degree 502), Frank Ewert (383), and Senthold Asseng (317), indicating a tightly knit collaborative group focused on crop modeling and climate impacts [E024].

The institution collaboration network (Figure 11) displays 72 nodes and 192 edges across 7 communities, drawn from 432 candidate institutions with at least 145 occurrences [E026, E062]. CNRS exhibits the highest weighted degree (10,514), followed by the Chinese Academy of Sciences (8,377), suggesting these institutions serve as global hubs. The network reveals a polycentric structure with distinct regional and thematic clusters, including a European-centered community anchored by CNRS and the Institut de Recherche pour le Développement, and an East Asian community centered on the Chinese Academy of Sciences and the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences [E026].

### 3.3 Conceptual Structure: Keywords and Thematic Evolution

The conceptual landscape of climate change research is dominated by three overarching keywords: "Climate change" (91,972 occurrences), "Geography" (90,947), and "Environmental science" (89,374) [E020]. These terms form the dense core of the keyword co-occurrence network (Figure 12), which contains 72 displayed nodes and 196 edges across 7 communities, derived from 432 candidate keywords with at least 513 occurrences [E027, E063].

The network reveals a well-differentiated thematic structure. Cluster 2, the largest and most central, encompasses the core terms "Climate change," "Geography," and "Environmental science," with "Climate change" alone exhibiting a weighted degree exceeding one million [E027]. Cluster 3 centers on "Ecology" (80,138 occurrences, weighted degree 854,889), reflecting the strong biogeophysical dimensions of climate research. Cluster 1 is organized around "Economics" (40,653 occurrences, weighted degree 464,987), indicating a substantial social-scientific component [E027].

Temporal analysis of keyword trends (Figure 9) demonstrates that all major thematic areas have grown in parallel with overall field expansion. "Climate change" as a keyword appeared in 87 documents in 1990 and 10,469 in 2021; "Geography" grew from 70 to 11,142; and "Environmental science" from 84 to over 5,000 by 2005 (the last year with complete data for this keyword in the evidence bundle) [E020]. The temporal overlay on the keyword network (Figure 16) indicates that the mean publication year for displayed keywords ranges from approximately 2013.6 to 2016.9 [E067], suggesting that the conceptual core represents relatively mature terminology, with emerging terms likely falling below the occurrence threshold for network inclusion.

The keyword density visualization (Figure 17) confirms the highest density in the "Climate change"–"Geography"–"Environmental science" nexus, with secondary density peaks in ecology and economics clusters [E068].

### 3.4 Intellectual Structure: Foundational Works and Knowledge Bases

The intellectual foundation of climate change research is revealed through cocitation, bibliographic coupling, and direct citation networks. The cocitation network (Figure 13) contains 72 displayed nodes and 192 edges across 5 communities, from a candidate pool of 432 references with at least 261 co-citations [E025, E064]. The most co-cited reference (weighted degree 56,343) is an OpenAlex-indexed work (W4285719527) with 19,138 occurrences, likely representing a major IPCC report or synthesis [E025]. Other highly co-cited works include additional OpenAlex-indexed references with weighted degrees exceeding 15,000, forming the intellectual backbone of the field [E025].

The bibliographic coupling network (Figure 15) connects 72 documents through 197 edges across 8 communities, based on shared references among 407 candidate documents with at least 10 occurrences [E022, E066]. The most central documents include "Investigating soil moisture–climate interactions in a changing climate: A review" (weighted degree 545), "Dryland climate change: Recent progress and challenges" (383), and "The broad footprint of climate change from genes to biomes to people" (371) [E022]. These works serve as integrative nodes, linking disparate research streams through their reference lists.

The direct citation network (Figure 14) is sparser, with 60 nodes and 55 edges across 10 communities, from 133 candidate documents with at least 456 citations [E023, E065]. The most cited work within this network is "Climate Change 2007: Impacts, Adaptation and Vulnerability," the IPCC Working Group II contribution to the Fourth Assessment Report, with 2,795 occurrences and a weighted degree of 51 [E023]. Other prominent nodes include the ENSEMBLES project summary and the PAGE2002 integrated assessment model documentation [E023]. This network confirms the central role of IPCC assessment reports as organizing frameworks for the field's intellectual structure.

### 3.5 Journal Concentration and Dissemination Patterns

Journal productivity follows a Bradford distribution, with a small core of journals contributing disproportionately to the literature. *Climatic Change* ranks first with 2,093 publications, followed by *The Science of the Total Environment* (1,708), *Sustainability* (1,545), and *Global Change Biology* (1,359) [E008, E009]. The Bradford analysis identifies 114 journals in Zone 1 (the core), contributing approximately 46,135 documents; 928 journals in Zone 2 (46,367 documents); and 15,883 journals in Zone 3 (46,273 documents) [E019]. This distribution indicates a mature field with a well-defined core of specialized journals, supplemented by a long tail of venues publishing climate-relevant research.

Notable among the top sources are repository-based outlets such as Zenodo (1,303 publications) and AgEcon Search (955), reflecting the inclusion of preprint and working paper repositories in the source database [E009]. High-impact interdisciplinary journals are also prominent, including *PLoS ONE* (1,201), *Scientific Reports* (710), and *Nature Climate Change* (704) [E009].

## 4. Discussion

The bibliometric evidence reveals a field that has undergone hyperexponential growth over three decades, with a compound annual growth rate of 17.57% [E006] that far exceeds typical scientific expansion rates. This growth trajectory reflects both the escalating urgency of climate change as a societal challenge and the institutionalization of climate research within funding agencies, assessment bodies, and academic structures.

The performance structure exhibits the skewness characteristic of bibliometric distributions. The gap between mean (49.56) and median (14.00) citations [E015], combined with 19.39% uncited works [E016], indicates that impact is concentrated among a relatively small corpus of highly influential publications. These foundational works—spanning food security [E028], biodiversity assessment [E029], soil carbon sequestration [E030], and species distribution modeling [E032]—have shaped the field's intellectual trajectory and continue to be heavily cited. The prominence of IPCC reports in the citation network [E023] underscores the symbiotic relationship between climate science and intergovernmental assessment processes: the IPCC both synthesizes and catalyzes research.

The social structure reveals an interesting duality. On one hand, the coauthorship network is dominated by a single author (AGB Poore) whose extraordinary publication count (5,808) [E010] is linked to a specific repository dataset [E021], representing a methodological artifact rather than individual research productivity in the traditional sense. On the other hand, the remaining top authors form coherent collaborative communities, particularly in agricultural impacts modeling [E024]. The institution collaboration network demonstrates a polycentric structure with strong European and East Asian hubs [E026], reflecting the global distribution of climate research capacity while also highlighting the dominance of well-resourced institutions in China, France, the United States, and Australia [E013].

The conceptual structure, as revealed by keyword co-occurrence, demonstrates that climate change research has achieved a high degree of thematic integration. The dense core of "Climate change," "Geography," and "Environmental science" [E027] serves as a conceptual nexus connecting ecology, economics, agriculture, public health, and energy research. The temporal overlay [E067] suggests that this core terminology stabilized in the mid-2010s, with newer thematic directions—such as climate finance [E051], climate anxiety [E041], and renewable energy transitions [E049]—emerging as distinct but connected research fronts.

The intellectual structure, mapped through cocitation and bibliographic coupling, confirms that climate change research rests on a foundation of synthesis reports, detection-attribution studies, and methodological advances in modeling and impact assessment. The bibliographic coupling network's central documents [E022] function as review and synthesis nodes, integrating knowledge across subfields and facilitating cross-disciplinary citation flows.

## 5. Limitations

Several limitations constrain the interpretation of these findings. First, the corpus is limited to journal articles as classified by the source database [E014]; books, book chapters, conference proceedings, and grey literature are excluded, potentially underrepresenting certain disciplinary contributions. Second, citation counts are source- and retrieval-date-dependent [E015 caveat] and do not account for differences in citation windows across publication years; recent works have had less time to accumulate citations [E016 caveat]. Third, network centrality measures describe structural positions within the constructed networks and must not be interpreted as indicators of causal or substantive importance [E022–E027 caveats]. Fourth, keyword frequencies depend on source metadata practices and synonym normalization [E020 caveat], and the abstract coverage of 74.73% [E003] limits text-mining depth. Fifth, the extraordinary publication count of the top-ranked author reflects repository indexing practices rather than individual authorship in the conventional sense [E021], and productivity rankings should not be equated with research quality [E009, E011, E013 caveats]. Sixth, the final publication year (2021) may be incomplete [E005 caveat], and the CAGR calculation does not establish monotonic trend [E006 caveat]. Finally, all findings are bounded by the source database's coverage, query formulation, and retrieval date [E002 caveat].

## 6. Conclusion

This bibliometric analysis of 151,562 climate change research articles published between 1990 and 2021 reveals a field characterized by extraordinary growth, concentrated impact, polycentric collaboration, and a mature, integrated conceptual structure. The performance structure demonstrates sustained exponential expansion, with a small core of journals and institutions contributing disproportionately to output and citation impact. The social structure features dense collaborative networks organized around methodological communities and regional hubs, with European and Chinese institutions serving as global connectors. The conceptual structure is anchored by a tightly integrated core of environmental, geographic, and ecological terminology, with distinct thematic clusters extending into economics, agriculture, health, and energy. The intellectual structure rests on foundational synthesis reports and landmark studies that continue to organize citation flows across the field.

Emerging research fronts in climate finance, renewable energy transitions, climate anxiety, and heat-related mortality [E041, E044, E049, E051] signal ongoing thematic diversification. As climate change research enters its fourth decade of accelerated growth, bibliometric monitoring will remain essential for understanding the field's evolving architecture and for identifying opportunities to strengthen interdisciplinary integration and knowledge translation.
