# CiteWeave: Auditable bibliometric synthesis with executable graph evidence and dependency-aware review

> Working draft v0.4 — 8 September 2026. The audited RQ1 graph experiments are reported; RQ2 human-participant outcomes and the confirmatory RQ3 same-evidence article comparison remain unavailable. This draft is therefore not submission-ready and intentionally makes no claim of demonstrated human–AI superiority. The related-work comparison has been expanded, but references, novelty coverage, ethics, and declarations still require author verification. Section headings are an editorial proposal for IP&M, not a verified mandatory journal template.

## 1. Introduction

Understanding a research field requires more than identifying individual relevant publications. Researchers also examine how topics develop, which groups collaborate, and how different bodies of work connect. Bibliometric analysis provides quantitative approaches to these questions through publication indicators and relational analyses of scholarly records. Its use nevertheless involves choices about the research question, data coverage, analytical techniques, and interpretation, rather than the mechanical production of a set of maps ([Donthu et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2021.04.070)). We focus on bibliometric synthesis: the construction of a written account of a research field using both computed bibliometric evidence and relevant source literature.

In a conventional workflow, researchers formulate a search strategy, obtain records from scholarly databases, resolve data inconsistencies, perform statistical and network analyses, inspect representative publications, and integrate the findings into a report. Established tools already automate substantial parts of this process. CiteSpace supports the investigation of temporal patterns in scientific literature; VOSviewer provides bibliometric mapping; and bibliometrix organizes data processing and science mapping within an extensible analytical workflow ([Chen, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.20317); [van Eck & Waltman, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-009-0146-3); [Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joi.2017.08.007)). These capabilities make analysis increasingly executable. Researchers must still establish how particular analytical outputs support the interpretations expressed in their writing and how changes to the analysis affect those interpretations.

Automation has subsequently expanded into literature retrieval and synthesis. PaperQA2 organizes literature search, cited summarization, and contradiction detection, while OpenScholar combines scientific retrieval with citation-supported responses and iterative feedback ([Skarlinski et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13740); [Asai et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.14199v1)). Longer-form systems extend automation to the organization of an article. AutoSurvey coordinates retrieval, outlining, section drafting, and revision, and LiRA assigns outlining, writing, editing, and reviewing to specialized agents ([Wang et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10252); [Go et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v40i47.41489)). The relevant research question is therefore no longer simply whether a system can retrieve documents and produce a structured review. It is how the resulting account remains connected to the evidence and transformations on which its claims depend.

This issue becomes particularly concrete for structural interpretations. Consider the claim that a topic connects otherwise separated research communities. Establishing that claim requires a specified corpus and graph construction, an operational definition of connection, and an appropriate computation. A stronger interpretation about dependence on a particular bridge may require recomputation after an edge or node is removed. Relevant passages can help interpret the result, but their retrieval does not by itself establish the corresponding graph property. Conversely, a correct graph calculation cannot establish the substantive meaning of a topic or justify a causal account of scientific development. Computational support and domain interpretation must be connected without treating either as a substitute for the other.

Graph-based retrieval and program-assisted reasoning offer complementary foundations for addressing this problem. GraphRAG uses graph organization and community summaries to support corpus-level synthesis, whereas program-aided language models delegate calculations to executable programs ([Edge et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130); [Gao et al., 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/gao23f.html)). Neither the presence of a graph nor the availability of a tool should be assumed to improve every task. Comparative GraphRAG research explicitly examines task-dependent benefits ([Xiang et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05690)). For bibliometric synthesis, this motivates distinguishing the retrieval of relevant evidence, the execution of structural analyses, and the expression of their results. It also motivates testing simple lookups alongside tasks requiring aggregation or structural perturbation.

Review introduces a further requirement. A reviewer may identify an incorrect entity, an unsuitable source, an invalid calculation, or an interpretation that exceeds the evidence. Such feedback may affect more than the sentence being reviewed. If several claims depend on the same evidence, correcting only the visible sentence can leave other parts of the report inconsistent. We therefore treat review as an operation on linked research objects as well as an assessment of text. Whether this organization improves correction quality or reduces review effort remains an empirical question; a review interface or a stored feedback history alone cannot establish either outcome.

We propose CiteWeave, a framework that connects protocol-driven bibliometric processing, executable graph evidence, evidence-conditioned writing, and dependency-aware review. Registered graph operations produce inspectable results that can be incorporated into bounded interpretations. These results are organized with source evidence and explicit limitations before being used in article synthesis. Review actions are linked to evidence and claims, allowing affected passages to be identified for controlled revision. Candidate feedback rules must pass support and held-out validation checks before being used beyond the original case. The aim is to make the basis and revision of a research interpretation inspectable, not to replace scholarly judgment with a graph score.

The proposed contribution has three parts: an explicit connection between bibliometric computations and written claims; a review mechanism that propagates changes through recorded evidence dependencies; and an evaluation design that separates structural task performance, reviewer-assisted correction, and article-level usefulness. These contributions do not entail a claim of a new graph algorithm, unrestricted autonomous planning, or demonstrated human–AI superiority. Section 2 positions the approach within the progression of research automation. Section 3 defines the task and research questions. The planned remaining sections describe the framework, evaluation design, results, discussion, and conclusions.

## 2. Related work

### 2.1. Bibliometric workflows and computational literature review

Bibliometric research combines performance-oriented indicators with analyses of relationships among publications, authors, institutions, and terms. Methodological guidance emphasizes aligning the selection of techniques with the purpose of the study and interpreting outputs in relation to the research domain ([Donthu et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jbusres.2021.04.070)). This establishes a useful distinction between carrying out a calculation and explaining what its result contributes to understanding a field.

Science mapping tools address substantial parts of that workflow. CiteSpace examines changes and emerging patterns through temporal analyses of scientific literature ([Chen, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1002/asi.20317)). VOSviewer concentrates on constructing and exploring bibliometric maps ([van Eck & Waltman, 2010](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11192-009-0146-3)). Bibliometrix supports an integrated analytical process and can be extended through the R ecosystem ([Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.joi.2017.08.007)). These systems should not be characterized as visualization-only tools or as lacking automation. The question pursued here concerns the explicit connection from their kinds of analytical outputs to generated claims and subsequent corrections.

Computational literature review also extends beyond conventional bibliometric networks. In IP&M, Chung et al. combine domain-specific, temporally updated language representations with clustering and emergingness measures to examine the evolution of AI research ([Chung et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ipm.2025.104245)). Their work illustrates how computational analysis can support a substantive account of a field. CiteWeave addresses a related but distinct processing problem: recording how particular analytical results and source materials support a written interpretation, and how a correction changes its downstream dependencies. This distinction does not imply that prior computational review methods are incapable of being incorporated into such a workflow.

### 2.2. Retrieval-based scholarly synthesis and automated review writing

Scientific retrieval systems have moved beyond returning a ranked list of papers. PaperQA2 evaluates an agent across literature question answering, cited topic summaries, and contradiction detection, including comparisons with human researchers ([Skarlinski et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.13740)). OpenScholar combines a scientific passage collection, retrieval, and a feedback-driven generation process, and evaluates both attribution and answer quality ([Asai et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.14199v1)). These studies establish that attribution, iterative synthesis, and human evaluation are existing research concerns, not features introduced by CiteWeave.

Other systems organize the preparation of a complete long-form document. STORM develops outlines through retrieval and questions informed by different perspectives; its evaluation includes feedback from experienced Wikipedia editors ([Shao et al., 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.347/)). AutoSurvey arranges literature acquisition, outline construction, subsection generation, and revision into a survey-writing process ([Wang et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10252)). LiRA uses specialized agents to organize, write, edit, and review scientific literature reviews ([Go et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v40i47.41489)). These approaches motivate treating planning and revision as substantive components of synthesis rather than relying on a single generation step.

SurveyGen provides a particularly relevant empirical baseline because it contains more than 4,200 human-written surveys and 242,143 cited references, and compares automatic with human-guided generation ([Bao et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.emnlp-main.136)). Its finding that fully automatic pipelines still exhibit weak citation quality and limited critical analysis motivates separating surface organization from claim support, domain-specific interpretation, and critical synthesis in our article evaluation. It also raises the standard for any comparison with human writing: a small convenience sample of unrelated articles can diagnose style differences, but cannot establish a same-evidence quality advantage.

The target tasks nevertheless differ. An answer to a scientific question, an encyclopedia-style article, a narrative literature review, and a bibliometric field analysis require overlapping but non-identical evidence. The present study focuses on statements whose support includes a transformation of a specified scholarly corpus, such as a community aggregation or a change in connectivity. The relevant comparison is therefore not whether another system can write a longer or better-organized article. It is whether comparable computational and source evidence can be used faithfully, traced to its origin, and revised consistently. Broad claims that existing writing agents have no provenance or no review mechanism would not be justified by these differences in task scope.

### 2.3. Graph-based retrieval and executable evidence

GraphRAG constructs an entity graph and community summaries to support questions about an entire collection of documents ([Edge et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.16130)). This moves beyond retrieving isolated passages by organizing information at several levels. However, an entity graph extracted from text is not identical to a bibliometric network: a coauthorship or co-occurrence edge has a specified construction rule, and its interpretation depends on that rule. Applying graph-based synthesis to bibliometrics consequently requires explicit treatment of edge semantics, graph scope, and analytical parameters.

GRAG retrieves textual subgraphs and supplies complementary text and graph views for multi-hop reasoning ([Hu et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.findings-naacl.232)). GraphRAG-Bench further evaluates graph construction, retrieval, reasoning coherence, and answers on domain-specific tasks that can require multiple hops, mathematics, or programming ([Xiao et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.02404)). Thus, neither presenting a graph beside text nor increasing question complexity is independently new. These studies instead sharpen the identification problem addressed here: graph-specific value should appear on tasks whose answer depends on a registered structural operation and should survive a comparison in which a flat condition receives the same computed answer. Our simple-versus-complex contrast, same-computation flat-program control, and raw-provenance ablation are designed for that narrower purpose.

Task-dependent comparisons are also essential. Xiang et al. investigate when graph-based retrieval is useful rather than assuming an advantage across all retrieval and generation settings ([Xiang et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.05690)). In our setting, a direct attribute lookup provides a useful contrast to a global aggregation or a deletion-based connectivity calculation. Matching their entities helps distinguish differences in computational demand from differences in subject matter. Comparisons must additionally account for the information and computational results made available to each condition.

Executable computation provides a separate point of comparison. PAL uses programs as intermediate representations and relies on an interpreter to obtain solutions ([Gao et al., 2023](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v202/gao23f.html)). CiteWeave does not introduce the general idea of outsourcing calculation to a program. It applies registered graph operations within a bibliometric evidence workflow and retains their outputs for synthesis and verification. The present benchmark supplies registered task programs; it does not establish that a language model independently discovers an appropriate analysis for an arbitrary research question. The methodological question is how computed evidence, underlying graph records, and source interpretation contribute to supported answers and articles.

### 2.4. Review, feedback, and human–AI collaboration

Review has both automated and human forms. PaperEval, published in IP&M, uses a multi-agent approach to assess papers along several aspects of novelty and originality ([Huang et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ipm.2025.104225)). LiRA likewise incorporates reviewing into its writing process ([Go et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1609/aaai.v40i47.41489)). These approaches are relevant to automated assessment, but the presence of a model acting as a reviewer must be distinguished from an independently observed human judgment.

Human–AI collaboration research also already considers whether to act automatically, collaborate with people, or defer a decision. LECODU jointly models collaboration and deferral to multiple users while considering the number of people involved and the associated cost ([Zhang et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.07003)). Consequently, routing an uncertain item to a reviewer is not, by itself, a sufficient novelty claim. CiteWeave's focus is the relationship between a review decision and the research objects affected by it: a source, graph result, claim, or passage.

Recent work makes this boundary still clearer. Learning-to-defer methods can adapt a policy to previously unseen experts from a small context set ([Tailor et al., 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v238/tailor24a.html)) and model dependence between human and model errors ([Wei et al., 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/wei24a.html)). HyPER explicitly optimizes routing between human and language-model preference labels ([Miranda et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.acl-long.355)), while Bayesian online consensus estimation trades human-query cost against posterior uncertainty when ground truth is unavailable ([Showalter et al., 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v238/showalter24a.html)). Reviewer capability estimation, cost-aware routing, and sequential querying therefore cannot be treated as isolated CiteWeave contributions. The proposed contribution is their use in a claim-evidence dependency system in which a review action has an explicit intervention target, propagates to registered dependants, and constrains the scope of revision.

Human involvement also does not automatically make an evaluation independent or reliable. In a preregistered annotation experiment, showing model suggestions changed label distributions without reducing annotation time and could inflate evaluations based on apparently human-approved labels ([Schroeder et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2025.findings-acl.1323)). A meta-analysis of 106 experiments likewise found that human–AI teams were, on average, worse than the better of the human-only and AI-only comparators, with substantial task heterogeneity ([Vaccaro et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-024-02024-1)). These findings motivate blind independent first judgments, separate human-only, AI-only, and human–AI conditions, immutable interaction logs, and direct measurement of quality, time, disagreement, and cost.

This focus leads to two distinct empirical issues. First, an upstream rejection should identify other explicitly dependent claims that need re-examination. Second, a correction should not automatically become a rule for unrelated studies. The framework therefore separates local revision from the validation of reusable feedback rules. Human correction quality, disagreement, effective review time, and transfer to held-out topics must be measured directly. Automated scoring, synthetic reviewers, and the number of stored feedback records cannot stand in for those measurements.

### 2.5. Positioning of the present study

The reviewed work demonstrates progress in analytical automation, scholarly retrieval, long-form writing, graph-based synthesis, executable reasoning, and review. CiteWeave investigates how these capabilities can be connected for a specific task. Its organizing unit is a research claim together with the data, computations, and source evidence supporting it and the dependencies that become relevant when it is corrected.

Three interfaces define the scope of the study: the transition from retrieved records to computed structural evidence; the transition from evidence to a bounded interpretation; and the transition from a review decision to consistent revision. This is a task-specific integration and evaluation argument, not an assertion that every related system lacks these capabilities. Establishing an advantage requires controlled comparisons at each relevant interface rather than treating the existence of an integrated pipeline as evidence of effectiveness.

## 3. Task formulation and research questions

### 3.1. Task and scope

The input to bibliometric synthesis consists of a research protocol, a bounded collection of scholarly records, graph-construction and analysis parameters, available source excerpts, and a writing brief. The output is a field-analysis report with links from empirical claims to supporting artifacts and a record of relevant analytical and review decisions. The task does not require reproducing an existing human publication, nor does it assume that bibliographic metadata and abstracts contain all evidence needed for a systematic review or a substantive causal explanation.

For example, an increase in the occurrence of a term supports a statement about that term within the sampled corpus. It does not alone establish that a technology has become more effective. Similarly, a change in graph connectivity after deleting a node describes a property of the constructed network. It is not an estimate of what would happen if a researcher, institution, or topic disappeared from the real world. These distinctions constrain both generation and review.

### 3.2. Relational graphs and evidence dependencies

We distinguish a relational graph from a derivation graph. A relational graph represents bibliometric entities and relationships under declared construction rules. A derivation graph records dependencies among sources, evidence artifacts, operator outputs, claims, and passages. Executing a registered analysis produces an evidence object whose interpretation is conditional on its inputs and parameters. Linking that object to a claim records a proposed support relationship; the existence of the link does not itself prove that the claim is semantically justified.

When an upstream object is invalidated, its recorded descendants can be marked for review. This provides a principled scope for controlled revision and helps preserve unaffected text. The guarantee is limited to represented dependencies: an unrecorded semantic reliance may remain undetected. Entity validity, computational correctness, claim support, and domain appropriateness therefore require distinct checks. Repeatable arithmetic on an incorrectly constructed graph cannot establish a valid scientific interpretation.

The graph experiment also has an explicit scope limitation. Graph-program contexts contain registered execution traces and selected supporting graph records, including support assembled from benchmark evidence. They are not treated as a demonstration of fully autonomous, non-oracle retrieval and planning. Query-driven and hierarchical retrieval conditions are evaluated separately, while operator-only and information-equivalent flat-program conditions help identify what is contributed by computation and access to underlying records. Registered large graphs are candidate-capped; results on them must not be described as results on untruncated entity graphs.

### 3.3. Research questions

The study is organized around three questions:

**RQ1. Under what task-complexity and graph-scale conditions does executable graph evidence improve structural answer quality and evidential support relative to retrieval-based alternatives?** This question separates simple lookups from aggregation, structural perturbation, and temporal–structural tasks. It also distinguishes evidence retrieval from the availability of computed results and from their serialization.

**RQ2. How does dependency-aware human review affect the correction of supported and unsupported claims, and what quality–effort trade-offs does it produce?** This question concerns both the immediate revision of affected passages and the validation of feedback beyond the reviewed case. Policy replay and intervention-based human comparisons have different inferential roles and should be reported separately.

**RQ3. Do the computational evidence and review mechanisms improve the support, traceability, and research usefulness of articles produced from the same evidence?** This question tests whether local task performance translates into useful scholarly synthesis. Traceability, domain specificity, and research usefulness are distinct outcomes; improvement in one does not establish improvement in all three.

Together, these questions evaluate the proposed interfaces without assuming that additional graph computation or human review is always beneficial. They organize the manuscript but do not replace or retrospectively alter the hypotheses and statistical decisions in the frozen experimental protocols.

## 4. The CiteWeave framework

### 4.1. Protocol-driven acquisition and canonical data

CiteWeave begins with a versioned research protocol rather than an unconstrained natural-language request. A protocol identifies the topic, date range, retrieval expression, graph types, graph-size conditions, task templates, and admissible interpretations. Retrieved records are normalized into canonical works and entities before graph construction. Input manifests bind the corpus, graphs, benchmarks, tokenizer, model profile, and execution plan by SHA-256. Subsequent corrections are introduced as dated amendments; earlier artifacts and failed attempts remain preserved. This design does not make a database sample complete or remove selection bias, but it makes the sample and transformations used for a result recoverable.

### 4.2. Executable graph evidence

The graph layer distinguishes record retrieval from structural computation. Registered tasks include weighted multi-hop paths across communities, global bridge selection followed by edge-deletion counterfactuals, contrasts between aggregate community importance and external-edge share, weighted-degree hub deletion followed by connectivity and replacement-hub recomputation, and joins between temporal growth and graph topology. A graph program invokes deterministic operators over a frozen graph and emits an operator trace, a structured answer, and identifiers for the underlying graph records. The language model is asked to synthesize these artifacts, not to approximate the graph calculation from a rendered image.

Three controls identify what the graph contributes. A hierarchical graph-retrieval condition receives graph-organized evidence without a complete executed answer. A flat-program condition receives the same computed content as the graph-program condition in a non-graph serialization. An operator-only condition receives the answer-bearing trace without the underlying graph provenance. The comparison therefore separates graph retrieval, external graph computation, serialization, and access to raw supporting records.

### 4.3. Evidence-conditioned synthesis

Computed phenomena are represented as evidence objects linking the task, operator trace, graph-evidence identifiers, relevant source excerpts, alternative explanations, and forbidden inferences. Long-form generation uses five such phenomena per article and three registered cross-phenomenon syntheses. A section dependency graph assigns evidence to Results, Discussion, and other sections. The graph-aware compiler differs from a matched flat compiler only by making these dependency edges explicit; both receive the same phenomena, source registry, synthesis membership, generation order, and word budget. Deterministic post-processing may delete complete sentences without evidence identifiers to meet a word budget, but it cannot alter the registered evidence-token set.

### 4.4. Dependency-aware review and controlled revision

Human review operates on claims and their visible dependencies. Article claims are sampled from Results and Discussion with coverage across phenomenon types and elevated priority for numerical, causal, multi-phenomenon, and interpretive statements. Reviewers record factual support, interpretive calibration, alternative explanations, evidence sufficiency, decisive evidence, invalid dependencies, a proposed action, and a rationale. The planned formal collection uses two independent primary reviewers and a third adjudicator only for disagreements. Server heartbeats measure active review time, while identity, timing, and submission fields are server-owned and frozen after submission.

An invalid source, phenomenon, or graph-evidence identifier is propagated through the explicit dependency graph to affected claims and paragraphs. Revision is local: changed paragraphs are hash-bound, unaffected text remains byte-preserved, and a replacement cannot introduce an unregistered evidence identifier. A prospective sequential value-of-information (VOI) policy additionally updates dependency-level Beta posteriors after each revealed decision and reallocates the remaining predicted-time budget toward claims with the greatest severity-weighted expected uncovered reach. Its fixed-policy comparators use the same propagation mechanism. A separate development server demonstrates live queue changes, including reopening an earlier reviewed claim when a later judgment invalidates a shared upstream dependency. This live mechanism is kept separate from confirmatory gold-label collection.

### 4.5. Feedback validation and audit controls

Feedback is not appended indiscriminately to later prompts. Local review actions first produce a dependency-scoped revision. Candidate reusable rules must pass support checks, held-out topic transfer, and unrelated-case regression gates before promotion to a retriever, planner, generator, or router component. Critical cases cannot be automatically accepted. Every experimental request has a canonical identity; transport failures are retained, parsing failures remain scored failures unless an identical registered retry is performed, and completed cells are not resampled. These controls constrain selective reporting but cannot replace external replication or human validation.

## 5. Evaluation design

### 5.1. Datasets, graph scope, and prospective amendments

The evaluation uses eight bibliometric topics: federated learning in healthcare, green-hydrogen electrolysis, perovskite solar-cell stability, spatial transcriptomics in cancer, explainable AI in medical imaging, desalination membrane fouling, organoid disease modelling, and sodium-ion battery cathodes. The first four formed the main panel and the latter four an independently frozen replication set. Topic graphs were evaluated at Small, Medium, and Large candidate caps of 100, 300, and 2,000 nodes. Amendments addressing entity identity, UTF-8 tokenization, nested operator budgets, neural dense retrieval, equal-dataset weighting, and complexity-matched negative controls were frozen before the corresponding outcomes were inspected.

### 5.2. Graph conditions and computational budgets

The main panel contains 100 tasks and eight conditions, yielding 800 model calls. The conditions are flat BM25, flat hybrid lexical/latent retrieval, BGE-M3 neural dense retrieval, query-driven graph retrieval, hierarchical graph retrieval, operator-only evidence, flat program, and graph program. Retrieval conditions share a fixed record budget; request construction uses an exact frozen tokenizer. Graph-program and flat-program requests contain the same computed answer information, while graph program retains the native provenance organization. Primary contrasts test graph program against flat hybrid (H1), hierarchical graph retrieval against flat hybrid (H2), and the Small-to-Large interaction in the graph-program advantage (H3).

### 5.3. Independent replication and complexity–scale analysis

The replication panel contributes 60 tasks and 120 graph-program/flat-hybrid calls across four new topics. A separate complexity extension matches 48 semantic anchors across simple and complex tasks and three scales. It contains 672 logical cells; 360 identity-equivalent cells are reused from frozen runs and 312 require new calls. The registered complexity contrast compares the graph-program versus neural-dense difference on complex and simple tasks, with a second contrast testing whether that selectivity increases from Small to Large. A 504-cell mechanism supplement compares graph program, flat program, and operator-only evidence over 168 tasks; 288 cells are frozen reuses and 216 are new calls.

### 5.4. Human review and controlled revision assessment

The human-review protocol requires six qualified reviewers, reviewer-by-domain-by-error calibration, blinded packets, server timing, double review, and independent adjudication. Four offline policies—uniform hash, static risk, static dependency, and sequential dependency VOI—will be replayed over the same complete resolved labels and the same pre-outcome predicted-time budgets at 25%, 50%, and 75% of always-review effort. The 50% budget is primary. Outcomes are actionable-claim capture and captured actionable claims per actual review minute; unresolved critical cases, abstention, and over-propagation are safety outcomes. Because no real reviewer return is currently available, this section defines an executable prospective analysis rather than a completed effectiveness experiment.

### 5.5. Same-evidence article assessment

The article experiment fixes, within topic, the same five graph phenomena, 15 source excerpts, writing brief, word range, and author access. Machine conditions use one response per registered request and cannot be resampled after a quality-gate failure. The intended confirmatory comparison includes graph-dependent generation, one-shot generation, and independent human authors, followed by blinded holistic ratings and two-reviewer assessment of 20 stratified claims per article. Research usefulness non-inferiority, traceability superiority, claim support, and domain-specificity non-degradation are distinct gates. The original 16 machine responses and subsequent compiler-development runs are reported as engineering outcomes because no complete 24-article, same-evidence human panel exists.

### 5.6. Outcomes, inference, failures, and reproducibility

Answer accuracy requires a valid structured response matching the registered answer set; evidence quality is measured by evidence F1. The independent unit for inference is the topic, not the number of tasks, claims, or reviewers. Main effects use equal-topic estimates, topic-cluster bootstrap intervals, and exact sign-flip tests. Registered families use Holm correction. With eight topics, the minimum attainable one-sided exact p-value is 1/256. Boundary mixed-logistic fits are reported only as diagnostics. Terminal audits require every planned cell exactly once and retain nonresponses or parsing errors rather than silently excluding them.

### 5.7. Human-participant ethics and research governance

No real human-review or same-evidence author outcome is included in the present results. Recruitment, consent, compensation, institutional ethics determination, conflicts, and data-sharing permissions must be completed and reported before RQ2 or RQ3 is presented as a human-participant result. The executable expert path now resolves condition-independent opaque evidence tokens to a shared, hash-bound viewer of graph answers, operator traces, interpretation limits, and source excerpts, and collects holistic, claim-level, and pairwise judgments with server-visible timing and immutable submission. Synthetic tests validate this software path only and are never counted as participants or judgments.

## 6. Results

### 6.1. Execution integrity

The main panel completed 800/800 calls, the independent replication completed 120/120 calls, and the complexity extension completed all 312 new calls and verified all 672 logical cells. The mechanism supplement completed 216/216 new calls and verified 504 logical cells after frozen reuse. Terminal audits found no duplicate primary cell and no terminal parsing failure. These counts establish execution completeness, not substantive superiority.

### 6.2. Main graph results

Across the 100-task main panel, answer accuracy was 0.00 for flat BM25, flat hybrid, and neural dense retrieval; 0.08 for query graph retrieval; 0.22 for hierarchical graph retrieval; 0.56 for operator-only evidence; 0.82 for flat program; and 0.88 for graph program. Graph program exceeded flat hybrid by 0.876 on the registered eligible set (topic-cluster bootstrap 95% interval 0.868–0.880; Holm-adjusted p = 1.55 × 10^-25). Hierarchical graph retrieval exceeded flat hybrid by 0.250 (95% interval 0.150–0.325; Holm-adjusted p = 3.81 × 10^-6). The registered Small-to-Large interaction was exactly 0 in all four main topics (p = 1.0).

### 6.3. Complexity, scale, and replication

The matched complexity extension changed the interpretation of the main effect. On 48 simple matched tasks, graph program, hierarchical graph retrieval, and neural dense retrieval all achieved 1.00 accuracy. On the 120 complex tasks, graph program achieved 1.00, hierarchical graph retrieval 0.28, and both flat hybrid and neural dense retrieval 0.00. The registered complexity-selectivity effect for graph program versus neural dense was +1.00 in every topic (Holm-adjusted exact p = 0.0078125). The graph-program advantage on simple tasks was equivalent to zero within ±0.05. Hierarchical retrieval also showed positive complexity selectivity, with an equal-topic estimate of 0.479 (95% interval 0.313–0.646).

Increasing the candidate cap did not further amplify the answer-level graph advantage. The scale-amplification estimate was 0 in all eight topics (p = 1.0), and the independent replication reproduced a zero Small-to-Large interaction in all four new topics. Thus task complexity, not graph size alone, selected the observed benefit under the tested caps and context budget.

### 6.4. Mechanism identification

Graph program and the information-equivalent flat program differed in accuracy by only 0.00595 (95% descriptive interval 0–0.0179) and met the registered ±0.05 equivalence criterion. Graph-shaped serialization was therefore not an independently detectable source of benefit. Relative to operator-only evidence, retaining raw graph provenance increased evidence F1 by 0.291 across topics (95% descriptive interval 0.280–0.308; Holm-adjusted exact p = 0.0078125) and increased answer accuracy by 0.20 while passing the registered non-inferiority gate. Raw provenance had no answer effect on four task types whose traces already exposed a derivable complete answer, but added +1.00 accuracy on the bridge-deletion task with a deliberately incomplete trace (Holm-adjusted exact p = 0.0078125). The evidence supports external deterministic graph computation plus provenance when the trace is insufficient; it does not support a claim that graph-formatted JSON is intrinsically superior or that the model autonomously discovered the graph program.

### 6.5. Article-generation diagnostics

All 16 frozen machine article requests returned a response, but all 16 failed the original quality gate. Every article exceeded the 2,700–3,300-word range. All eight graph-conditioned responses reached the 8,000 completion-token ceiling; six lacked a complete Conclusion, two of those also lacked Limitations, and one contained a truncated unregistered phenomenon identifier. A post-generation parser audit recovered nested Results and Discussion content without reclassifying the original failures. It showed all five phenomena in both sections for 16/16 drafts and all three registered cross-phenomenon syntheses in 8/8 graph drafts, compared with at least two syntheses in 4/8 one-shot drafts. This is structural coverage, not a quality victory.

A matched two-topic compiler development produced four 2,835–3,187-word drafts with 23–38 reviewable claims, and all four passed claim-packet readiness. Graph-minus-flat claim counts were +15 and +3 across the two topics. However, nine of 36 section calls ended at the token limit, two drafts contained truncated evidence identifiers, and 0/4 met the joint strict gate. A deterministic comparison of these four drafts with eight cached published references found unusually uniform machine paragraph length (mean coefficient of variation 0.15 versus 0.71) and more calibration markers (6.52 versus 2.15 per 1,000 words), but fewer numerical mentions (18.22 versus 48.38 per 1,000 words). Because topics, evidence, and source genres differ, these values diagnose templated expansion and evidence density; they do not compare factual quality. The token-safe matched retry remains at 0/36 valid responses because the configured provider account reports no available balance.

### 6.6. Human-review status

The dependency propagation, local revision, capability routing, offline sequential VOI replay, and development live-routing paths pass deterministic and synthetic integration tests. A development feature panel contains 80 claims, 132 article-scoped dependencies, and a maximum within-article dependency fanout of 20. No real primary reviewer, adjudication, live VOI outcome, or independent expert article rating has been collected. RQ2 and the human-comparative part of RQ3 therefore remain unanswered.

## 7. Discussion

The strongest finding is conditional rather than universal: executable graph evidence was useful when a task required non-local computation, but it added nothing on matched simple queries that neural retrieval already solved. Enlarging the graph did not magnify the answer-level effect. This directly cautions against treating graph size as a sufficient recipe for demonstrating GraphRAG value. The benchmark had to require global selection, aggregation, interventions, recomputation, and temporal–structural joins before graph computation became identifiable.

The mechanism study narrows the claim further. Once computed content was held constant, graph and flat serialization were equivalent. The contribution is therefore not a preferred prompt shape. It is the availability of verified external computation and, when the answer-bearing trace is incomplete, the underlying provenance needed to check and complete the claim. This distinction also explains why the graph-program result should not be labelled unrestricted model graph reasoning: for most complex items, the registered trace already exposed the answer fields that a deterministic program had produced.

Local task accuracy did not automatically translate into publishable articles. Explicit dependencies increased cross-phenomenon coverage and the number of reviewable claims, but long-form generation was vulnerable to repetition, uniform paragraph structure, overlength, and truncation. The graph condition could simultaneously encourage stronger interpretation and more cautious wording without increasing domain-specific evidence. A successful article experiment must consequently hold evidence and length constant and evaluate claim support, domain specificity, traceability, and research usefulness separately.

The review architecture addresses a substantive systems question: a later human discovery can invalidate multiple claims, including a claim that was accepted earlier. Dependency propagation, reopening, local hash-bound revision, and sequential VOI make this state change explicit instead of storing feedback as an undifferentiated prompt history. Nevertheless, no human-effectiveness claim follows from a functioning implementation. The registered offline replay still requires complete independent labels and frozen time estimates, while a causal live-effort claim would require a separately randomized deployment.

Several limitations constrain generalization. The eight topics are the inferential clusters, and floor/ceiling separation makes some observed effects unusually large. Graphs are candidate-capped constructions rather than exhaustive representations of the fields. Entity normalization and edge semantics remain upstream sources of error. The tasks use registered programs and do not measure autonomous task decomposition. Results come from one provider/model profile and a fixed context regime. Explicit dependency propagation cannot discover unrecorded semantic dependencies. Finally, the absence of real reviewers and a qualified same-evidence human article panel prevents conclusions about human–AI complementarity, labor savings, or article quality relative to experts.

## 8. Conclusion

The completed evidence answers RQ1 but not RQ2 or RQ3. Across eight bibliometric topics, deterministic graph programs produced a large, perfectly topic-consistent advantage on complex structural tasks and no advantage on matched simple tasks; increasing the candidate cap did not amplify the effect. Same-computation controls showed that graph serialization itself was equivalent to a flat representation, whereas raw provenance was valuable when an answer-bearing trace was incomplete. CiteWeave therefore supports a bounded claim: registered graph computation and provenance can make complex bibliometric synthesis more answerable and auditable under explicit task and graph definitions.

The system also implements dependency-scoped review, controlled revision, and outcome-adaptive routing, but their benefit remains a prospective human-participant question. Article generation shows promising structural synthesis alongside clear completion and stylistic failures. A submission that presents the full three-part contribution must wait for real review outcomes and the same-evidence expert comparison; otherwise the manuscript should be scoped to the completed graph-computation study and treat oversight and article generation as future evaluation.
